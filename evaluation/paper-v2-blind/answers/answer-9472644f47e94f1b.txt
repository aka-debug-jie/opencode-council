**Decision.** The three papers do not contradict one another; they answer different questions under different premises. The genuinely unresolved question for this service is: with incomplete knowledge of which invariants need global enforcement, no supplied paper gives a decision procedure for choosing among the three defaults — always-coordinate (Paper-1), deterministic-order-without-2PC (Paper-2), coordinate-only-if-not-I-confluent (Paper-3) — nor for bounding the cost of choosing wrong on hot records under partitions.

**Evidence and assumptions.** Paper-1 argues it is "better to have application programmers deal with performance problems due to overuse of transactions… rather than always coding around the lack of transactions" (§2.3, p.4), always pays TrueTime+Paxos+2PC (§4.1.2, pp.6–7), and reports a write-latency "fat tail due to lock conflicts" on hot records (Table 6, p.11). Paper-2 claims deterministic ordering lets it "completely eschew distributed commit protocols" and shrink the "contention footprint" (§1.1–1.3, pp.1–2), but it requires declared read/write sets (dependent transactions need OLLP re-checks, §3.2.1, p.4), and hot records still serialize within the global order (§6.3, pp.9–10). Paper-3 proves coordination is necessary iff invariants+operations are not I-confluent (Theorem 1, §4.2, p.189) and states that incomplete/incorrect invariants force fallback to serializable transactions (§1, p.185; §4.3, p.190). The hidden assumption (my inference): each paper assumes knowledge this team lacks — Paper-3 assumes complete invariants; Paper-2 assumes deterministic, fully declared transactions; Paper-1 assumes coordination cost is always acceptable. "Coordination is necessary" is relative to that epistemic premise, so no contradiction follows.

**Alternatives.** (A) Paper-1: always coordinate, which preserves even unknown invariants but pays hot-record serialization, WAN RTT, and partition unavailability. (B) Paper-2: move coordination upstream to one global order and skip per-commit 2PC; cost is sequencing latency plus stale or undeclared read/write sets. (C) Paper-3: coordinate only non-I-confluent pairs — e.g., a "balance ≥ 0" invariant with a withdrawal is non-I-confluent (Table 2, "> Decrement: No", p.191) — but C is inapplicable without invariants. A and B differ in where coordination lives (per-commit agreement vs. upfront ordering), not whether any ordering exists.

**Failure modes.** A: hot records → lock-conflict fat tail. B: wrong read/write sets → OLLP restarts; nondeterministic dependent transactions break the determinism guarantee. C: a missed invariant → silent corruption (negative balances) — exactly this team's risk.

**Falsification tests.** New proposal — "invariant elicitation + divergence replay," not a head-to-head benchmark. (1) Elicit candidate invariants cheaply: scan schema DDL and code for assertions/balance/limit checks and interview owners. (2) Replay one week of production writes on a coordination-free shadow using merge semantics (set-union inserts, commutative counters) and test candidate invariants on converged states. Why it could work: Paper-3's Table 2 shows the dividing line is per invariant+operation pair, so empirically observing which pairs a coordination-free merge violates proxies for I-confluence without a full proof. What would falsify it: if replay finds no violations yet production later corrupts, the invariant set was incomplete — falsifying the "invariants are knowable" premise (then adopt A). If violations cluster on hot records, the bottleneck is ordering/serialization, not 2PC RTTs, distinguishing A's fat tail from B's ordering skew. Cheapest check: the DDL/code scan plus a 30-minute owner interview; the replay is a few engineer-days on existing logs.

**Open questions.** Can partial invariants be soundly "escalated" (coordinate only uncertain pairs)? Is invariant mining complete enough to satisfy Paper-3's premise? Would Paper-1's clock-uncertainty API reduce Paper-2's sequencer availability risk? None is answered in the supplied papers.
